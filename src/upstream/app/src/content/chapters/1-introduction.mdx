<Fragment set:html={"<p class=\"full\">What an MoE offers lies in how it spends its inference compute. Turning that inference saving into faster training is the hard part.</p><p class=\"full\">Our previous deep dive on training, the <a href=\"https://huggingface.co/spaces/HuggingFaceTB/smol-training-playbook\" target=\"_blank\" rel=\"noopener\">Smol Training Playbook</a>, covered training a dense model but only touched on MoE design. Because the star of that book, SmolLM3, was a dense transformer, its training needed no technique specific to MoE, like <a href=\"https://huggingface.co/spaces/nanotron/ultrascale-playbook?section=expert_parallelism\" target=\"_blank\" rel=\"noopener\">expert parallelism</a>. Still, we glanced at MoE in the playbook&#x27;s &quot;Going Sparse: MoE&quot; section to cover its high-level design choices: sparsity, granularity, shared experts and load balancing. That section named the main reasons distributed MoE training is complex: expert placement, load balancing and all-to-all communication, but without diving into the particulars<sup data-k=\"stp-dense\"><a href=\"#ref-doc-0\" data-footnote-ref=\"\">2</a></sup>. This handbook starts from that list.</p><p class=\"full\">Going from a dense model to an MoE gives you three new challenges to manage: a router that decides which experts each token uses, a load that has to stay balanced across the experts, and, once the experts are split across GPUs, an <a href=\"https://github.com/NVIDIA/Megatron-LM/blob/d6316eb45bf1bfbb6f1f848c0b56e55166564d80/megatron/core/transformer/moe/token_dispatcher.py#L708-L727\" target=\"_blank\" rel=\"noopener\">exchange of tokens</a> between those GPUs in every MoE layer.
